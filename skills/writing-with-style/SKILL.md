# Writing with Style

Apply these principles from Steven Pinker's "The Sense of Style" to produce clear, engaging prose.

**Punctuation override (user preference, wins over any Pinker advice below):**
never write the `<claim>: <elaboration>` sentence shape, i.e., a colon
introducing a clause that expands the claim before it. Reserve colons for
lists and definitions. Rewrite as two sentences, or use "e.g.," / "i.e.," /
"that is" / "namely". Avoid em-dashes and semicolons in prose likewise.

The em-dash ban and the colon ban interact, and this is where the rule
usually breaks. The colon is the nearest substitute for an em-dash, so
removing one tends to produce the other. Both are banned.

✗ "This paper asks a practical deployment question: when an organization runs
a local agent to diagnose an outage, does thinking mode help or hurt?"
✓ "This paper asks whether thinking mode helps or hurts a local agent
diagnosing an outage."
✓ "The verifier checks three predicates: existence, provenance, and fidelity."
(a list, so the colon is correct)

**Diction override (same authority):** never use "inert", "headline"
(adjective), "load-bearing", "rungs", or "conflates". Limit hard, at most
one per paragraph: "rather than" (prefer "instead of"), the contrast
appositive "X, not Y", the connective ", so " (prefer "therefore",
"hence", "thus", "which means"), and negation-heavy hedging that defines
things by what they are not (prefer stating what IS true; keep a negation
only when the negative claim is itself the point).

## Learning to Write

The path to good writing starts with reading, not rules.

### Be a Good Reader First:
- Good writers absorb "a vast inventory of words, idioms, constructions, tropes, and rhetorical tricks" from reading widely
- Develop "a sensitivity to how they mesh and how they clash"
- Writers acquire technique by "spotting, savoring, and reverse-engineering examples of good prose"
- Skills come from reading other writers, not from style books

### Reverse-Engineer Good Prose:
- Linger over writing that strikes you as excellent
- Reflect on what makes it good
- Notice patterns: word choice, sentence structure, rhythm, metaphor
- Biographers of great authors always try to identify what books shaped them

### The Wrong Way to Learn:
- Traditional style advice is too negative: "Never use," "Avoid," "Never split"
- Classic manuals try to "take all the fun out of writing"
- This crushes the motivation that must "first be kindled by a delight in the best work of the masters"
- Learning should feel like "a form of pleasurable mastery, like cooking or photography"

## Why Style Matters

Three reasons to care about writing well:

1. **Communication**: Clear style prevents wasted time and errors
   - Poor writing causes "death on the highway caused by a badly worded road sign"
   - Clear language prevents "vast amounts of error, frustration, and waste"

2. **Trust**: Readers who see care in your prose will trust your care in other matters
   - A tech executive: "If it takes someone more than 20 years to notice how to properly use it's, that's not a learning curve I'm comfortable with"
   - Sloppy grammar signals sloppy thinking

3. **Beauty**: "A crisp sentence, an arresting metaphor, a witty aside, an elegant turn of phrase are among life's greatest pleasures"
   - Appreciating beauty motivates the work of learning to write well

## Classic Style: The Core Philosophy

**Prose is a window onto the world.** The writer has seen something the reader hasn't noticed; the writer orients the reader's gaze so they can see it for themselves.

### The Classic Style Stance:
- **Purpose**: Presentation of truth, not persuasion
- **Motive**: Disinterested truth (even if you have an agenda, don't show it)
- **Writer's role**: Show, don't argue
- **Reader assumption**: Competent, can recognize truth when shown
- **Communication model**: Conversation, not lecture
- **Writer and reader**: Equals in an intimate discussion

### What Classic Style Is NOT:
- **Self-conscious style**: Drawing attention to the writing itself
- **Practical style**: Instructions, manuals
- **Contemplative style**: Musing, introspecting
- **Prophetic style**: Oracular pronouncements

### Classic Style Is a Pretense:
- Writers must "visualize ourselves in some kind of conversation, or correspondence, or oration, or soliloquy"
- **Pretend** that your thoughts were fully formed before writing (they weren't)
- Like a celebrity chef pulling a perfect soufflé from the oven in the final minute—the messy work was done beforehand
- **The goal**: Make it seem as if clarity came naturally, not from revision and toil

### The Conversation Test:
- Would you say this to someone face-to-face?
- In conversation, we don't announce our intentions: "I'm going to say three things..."
- We just say them
- If signposting sounds unnatural aloud, it's too heavy

### Exact Analogies, Not Loose Metaphors:
- Good writers use analogies that are **actual instances** of the phenomena being explained
- "Division by zero" is a perfect example of "equations breaking down" (not just similar)
- "Gravity tugging at a tossed ball" works exactly the same way as cosmic expansion slowing
- Exact analogies let readers verify understanding themselves

## Opening Strong

Start with something worth reading.

### What NOT to Do:
- Avoid clichés: "Since the dawn of time," "Recently, scholars have been increasingly concerned with..."
- Avoid banalities: stating the obvious or standard claims
- Don't start with your research process

### What TO Do:
- Open with a **contentful observation that provokes curiosity**
- Lead with what you found, not how you found it
- Use paradox to draw readers in: "We're lucky because we'll die?" (Who wouldn't want to find out?)
- Start with a question that makes readers want an answer

### Rescue Superlatives from Cliché:
- "Incredible," "awesome," "amazing" are inflated by overuse
- Find fresh alternatives: Dawkins uses "stupefying" (to render into a stupor)
- Fresh superlatives still carry power because they're less worn

### Use Concrete Images:
- Open with something readers can visualize
- "We are going to die, and that makes us the lucky ones" (visceral, paradoxical)
- Not "the statistical improbability of existence" (abstract)

## Defeating the Curse of Knowledge

The main cause of bad writing is the difficulty of imagining what it's like for someone who doesn't know what you know.

### Strategies to Break the Curse:
1. **Show a draft to someone** who is like your intended reader
2. **Read aloud** - hear where readers will stumble
3. **Look for jargon** - technical terms you've forgotten are technical
4. **Unpack abstractions** - turn nominalizations back into verbs and specifics
5. **Add examples** - concrete instances of abstract points
6. **Use analogies** - bridge from known to unknown

### Signs You're Cursed:
- Using abbreviations without defining them
- Referring to findings without explaining them
- Assuming background knowledge readers lack
- Chunking information into expert-level units

## Word Choice

### Fresh Over Familiar:
- Avoid clichés and stock phrases
- Seek words that are *telling* - that capture exactly the right nuance
- **Phonesthetics**: Best words echo meaning in their sound
  - Good: "haunting" (sounds haunting), "tart" means tart, "voluptuous" has voluptuous sounds
  - Bad: "pulchritude" (sounds ugly, means beauty)
- "It's no coincidence that haunting means 'haunting' and tart means 'tart'"

### When to Use Uncommon Words:
- Against a backdrop of simple nouns and verbs
- When they convey exactly the right meaning
- "A skilled writer can enliven and sometimes electrify her prose with the judicious insertion of a surprising word"
- Not to impress, but to illuminate
- Read with dictionary at hand; use words readers may not know but will see again

### Avoid Polysyllabic Latinisms:
- "cessation" for "end," "eventuate in" for "cause"
- "is contributive to" instead of "contributes to"
- These are genuinely bad—they add nothing

### When Uncommon Words ARE Valuable:
- Varied vocabulary and unusual words distinguish "sprightly prose from mush"
- "Bibulous" (tending to drink too much) calls to mind babbling, bobbling, bubbling—phonesthetically alive
- Use a thesaurus but remember the bicycle repair manual warning: "Do not get carried away with the destructive potential of this tool"

### Avoid:
- Metaconcepts: "approach," "assumption," "concept," "condition," "context," "framework," "issue," "level," "model," "perspective," "process," "role," "strategy," "tendency"
- Zombie nouns (nominalizations): "The decision was made" → "We decided"
- Hedging for no reason: "somewhat," "rather," "to some extent"

### When Zombie Nouns ARE Actually Useful:
- **Second reference**: "The governor canceled the convention. The cancellation surprised everyone." (honors given-new)
- **To maintain topic flow**: Put familiar nominalization first, new info last
- **When the event itself is the topic**: "The investigation revealed corruption"

### Intensifiers Often Weaken:
- "He's honest" → implies completely honest (categorical)
- "He's very honest" → creates a scale (8.7 out of 10, not 10/10)
- Unmodified adjectives feel stronger and more absolute
- Save intensifiers for genuine amplification: "very rare" makes sense because "rare" has a natural scale

### Scare Quotes (Shudder Quotes):
**Legitimate uses:**
1. Reproducing someone else's exact words
2. Mentioning a word as a word: "The Times uses 'millenniums,' not 'millennia'"
3. Signaling non-acceptance of meaning in context: They violated their family's "honor"

**NOT legitimate:**
- Squeamishness about your own word choice
- Distancing yourself from common idioms: "think outside the 'box'"
- **If you're not comfortable using an expression without apologetic quotation marks, you probably shouldn't be using it at all**

## Sentence Structure

### Put Heavy After Light:
- Light → Heavy (easier to parse)
- Short subjects, longer predicates
- "Susan gave the struggling student who had failed the first exam a passing grade" (hard)
- "Susan gave a passing grade to the struggling student who had failed the first exam" (easier)

### Right-Branching Over Left-Branching:
- **Left-branching** (harder): "The dog the cat the rat bit chased fled"
- **Right-branching** (easier): "The rat bit the cat that chased the dog that fled"

### Avoid Monotony After Parallel Structures:
- After using parallel constructions multiple times, **vary the structure**
- Parallel: "never going to die... never going to be born"
- Then vary: "been here in my place... see the light of day"
- This prevents tedium and maintains reader interest

### Managing Complexity:
- Break long sentences into shorter ones
- Use sentence order to signal structure (see punctuation override above)
- Put explanatory material after the main clause, not before

### Avoid Center-Embedding:
Don't jam phrases into the middle of other phrases:
- **Bad**: "The reporter everyone I met trusts said the program failed"
- **Good**: "Everyone I met trusts the reporter who said the program failed"
- **Sign of trouble**: Reader accumulates similar words before resolving the structure

### Avoid Garden Path Sentences:
Garden paths are syntactic ambiguities that force readers to backtrack and re-parse:

**Classic examples:**
- "The horse raced past the barn fell" (The horse that was raced past the barn fell)
- "Fat people eat accumulates" (Fat that people eat...)
- "When Fred eats food gets thrown" (When Fred eats, food...)

**Real-world example:**
- **Garden path**: "During the primary season, Mr. Romney opposed the Dream Act, proposed legislation that..."
  (Did Romney propose it? No, it's proposed legislation)
- **Fixed**: "During the primary season, Mr. Romney opposed the Dream Act, which is proposed legislation that..."

**How to prevent:**
- Add signal words early: "that," "which," "who"
- Use punctuation: commas, dashes to signal structure
- Read aloud after time has passed (curse of knowledge hides these)

## Analogies and Examples

### The Power of Exact Analogies:
- Use analogies that are actual instances of phenomena, not loose metaphors
- Brian Greene explains multiverse theory using a shoe store analogy:
  - "Just as a well-stocked shoe store guarantees you'll find your size, only a well-stocked multiverse can guarantee our universe will be represented"
  - This is an exact correspondence, not a vague similarity

### Concrete Details Over Abstractions:
- Replace abstract language with concrete, visualizable specifics:
  - Not "denial of economic opportunities" but "Typists wanting to work in an office"
  - Not "oppression" but "Yard boys scared a single gesture near the planter's wife could leave them hanging from an oak tree"
  - Not "the Dust Bowl" but "when the land turned to dust"

### Force Active Mental Visualization:
- "Fresh wording and concrete images force us to keep updating the virtual reality display in our minds"
- Wilkerson: "as hard and unyielding as the red Georgia clay"
  - Sensory image with allusion (MLK's "red hills of Georgia") and lyrical meter
  - Prose brought to life with a snatch of poetry

## Coherence: Arcs of Connection

### The Given-New Contract:
1. Start with what the reader knows (given)
2. Add what the reader doesn't know (new)
3. The new becomes given for the next sentence

### Topic and Comment:
- Each sentence has a **topic** (what it's about) and a **comment** (what you say about it)
- Topics should connect from sentence to sentence
- Keep topics consistent through a paragraph

### Coherence Relations:
| Type | Function | Markers |
|------|----------|---------|
| **Elaboration** | Add detail | specifically, in particular, for example |
| **Similarity** | Draw parallel | similarly, likewise |
| **Contrast** | Show difference | but, however, in contrast |
| **Cause-Effect** | Show causation | so, because, as a result |
| **Temporal** | Show sequence | then, later, afterward |

### Use Connectives Wisely:
- Don't overuse "however" and "therefore"
- Let logical connections speak for themselves when clear
- Use connectives when the relation isn't obvious

### State Your Point Early:
Don't force readers through your research journey:
- **The PhD thesis cartoon**: Firing arrow, then painting target around it
- That's not how science works, but it IS how writing must work
- Readers care about your **subject**, not your **research process**
- Lead with what you found, not how you found it

### Vision Metaphor Replaces Metadiscourse:
- Instead of: "The preceding paragraph demonstrated that..."
- Write: "As we have seen, parents sometimes give a boy's name to a girl, but never vice versa"
- Since seeing implies seers, use "we" for writer and reader looking at spectacle together

## Signposting

### The Problem with Rigid Signposting:
Comes from classical rhetoric: "Say what you're going to say, say it, then say what you've said"
- Makes sense for long orations (listeners can't backtrack)
- Less necessary in writing where readers can reread
- Intrusive in classic style, which simulates conversation

### The Shortcut Analogy:
"Complicated directions for a shortcut which take longer to figure out than the time the shortcut would save"

### Natural vs. Forced Signposting:
**Natural** (from conversation):
- "Let me tell you a story"
- "To make a long story short"
- "In other words"
- "As I was saying"
- "Did you hear the one about..."

**Forced** (to avoid):
- "I will now discuss X"
- "This section will examine Y"
- "In the following paragraphs, we will see..."

### Better Approaches to Organization:
1. **Lay the route clearly** so every turn is obvious when readers get to it
2. **Take advantage of reader's expectations** of where to go next
3. Organize logically: general to specific, big to small, early to late
4. Tell a story with a narrative arc

### Replacing Metadiscourse with Movement:
**Instead of:**
> "The previous section analyzed the source of word sounds. This section raises the question of word meanings."

**Write:**
> "Now that we have explored the source of word sounds, we arrive at the puzzle of word meanings."

**Instead of:**
> "The first topic to be discussed is proper names."

**Write:**
> "Let's begin with proper names."

### Writing Good Summaries:
- Don't just copy sentences and paste at end (confesses you're shuffling words)
- Repeat key words to connect back to earlier passages
- Fit words into **new sentences** that work as coherent passage
- Make it "almost as if the material being summarized had never existed"

## Comparisons and Parallelism

### The Rule of One Variable:
In experiments: Change one variable, hold everything else constant.

In writing: To show a difference, vary only the words expressing that difference—keep everything else identical.

**Bad (varying syntax):**
> "In ten nations with largest online populations, non-domestic news sites represent <8% of most-visited sites, while in France, 98% of visits to news sources are directed to domestic sites."

**Good (parallel syntax):**
> "In ten nations with largest online populations, non-domestic news sites represent <8% of most-visited sites; in France, the figure is just 2%."

### Avoid Synonymomania:
Don't capriciously vary terms in comparisons and contrasts:
- **Bad**: "Herons are opportunistic hunters, but great blues defend fishing holes when frozen"
  (Are only great blue herons territorial? Or all herons?)
- **Good**: "Herons are opportunistic hunters, but herons defend fishing holes when frozen"
- Readers assume different words = different things, even if unintended
- **Exception**: When synonym is genuinely more generic ("the vehicle" after "the bus")

## Show, Don't Tell

### Telling Details:
- Use concrete specifics that imply the general point
- Don't say someone is "brilliant" — show what they did
- Details should *obviate* the explicit pronouncement
- Make the little girl endearing not by saying "cute" or "adorable" but by showing her childlike mannerisms

### Examples:
- **Telling**: "The party was boring"
- **Showing**: "People checked their phones every few minutes, and by nine o'clock half the guests had left"

### Avoid Euphemisms When Directness Matters:
- "The sister whose hand I am clutching in the picture is dead"
- No euphemism—"has passed away," "is no longer with us"—could have the same impact
- Euphemisms undermine analysis before it begins

### Zeugma: Juxtaposing Different Word Senses:
- "Roundly praised, intermittently censored, and occasionally eaten"
- "Books" used in sense of content (praised, censored) AND physical form (eaten)
- Subtly teases readers while maintaining clever understatement

### Surprising Transitions:
- Pinker praises surprising transitions (colons, dashes, block quotations); the punctuation override above still applies, so achieve the surprise with sentence order and block quotations, not colons or dashes
- Instead of plodding "Here is another example..." interrupt narration without warning
- "A writer, like a cinematographer, manipulates the viewer's perspective with the verbal equivalent of camera angles and quick cuts"

## Managing Complexity

### Multiple Negations Overwhelm:
- Each negative taxes working memory
- Two negatives: manageable
- Three or more: overwhelming for readers
- **Bad**: "It is not impossible the plan will not be rejected"
- **Good**: "The plan might be accepted"

### Hedging with Proportion:
- Match hedging to claim importance
- Don't hedge obvious truths ("somewhat frequently" when you mean "often")
- Do hedge bold claims appropriately ("may," "could," "tends to")

### Compulsive Hedging (CYA Writing):
Common hedge words: almost, apparently, comparatively, fairly, in part, nearly, partially, predominantly, presumably, rather, relatively, seemingly, so to speak, somewhat, sort of, to a certain degree, to some extent

**Why writers hedge:**
- Hope to get off the hook if a critic tries to prove them wrong
- Same reason journalists use "allegedly" and "reportedly" excessively

**The Alternative: Common Sense**
- Classic writers count on readers' common sense and ordinary charity
- In conversation: "Liz wants to move out of Seattle because it's a rainy city"
  - You don't interpret this as claiming it rains 24/7
  - No need for "relatively rainy" or "somewhat rainy"

**Key principle:**
> "It's not that good writers never hedge their claims. It's that their hedging is a choice, not a tic."

### When Precision IS Required:
1. **Legal documents**: Will be interpreted adversarially
2. **Scholarly hypotheses**: Must be stated precisely so critics can evaluate them
3. **Statistical tendencies**: When readers might misinterpret as absolute laws

**Better approach:**
- Use explicit magnitudes: "During the twentieth century, democracies were half as likely to go to war with each other as autocracies were"
- Not: "Democracies don't fight wars"

## Endings and Rhythm

### Strong Endings:
- End sentences with the most important word
- End paragraphs with the most important sentence
- **Good writing finishes strong**

### The Power of Short Paragraphs:
- Wilkerson ends a richly descriptive chapter with: "They left."
- Two syllables, enormous impact
- "The abrupt ending and the expanse of blankness at the bottom of the page mirror the finality of the decision to move and the uncertainty of the life that lay ahead"

### Against Rigid Paragraphing Rules:
- "Among the many dumb rules of paragraphing foisted on students is the one that says a paragraph may not consist of a single sentence"
- One-sentence (or two-word) paragraphs are powerful when used deliberately

### Rhythm:
- Vary sentence length
- Short sentences for key claims, but never under 6 words. Merge a
  too-short claim into a neighboring sentence instead of leaving a fragment
  like "Conditioning granularity proves decisive."
- Longer sentences for elaboration
- Single-sentence paragraphs CAN work for rhetorical effect
- Meter and sound should resonate with meaning and mood

## Clichés and Mixed Metaphors

### Clichés Break the Spell:
> "Classic prose is a pleasant illusion, like losing yourself in a play. The writer must work to keep up the impression that prose is a window onto the scene rather than just a mess of words."

- A writer relying on clichés is like "an actor with a wooden delivery"
- Reader stops converting language into mental images
- Slips back into just "mouthing the words"

### Why Clichés Fail:
- The cliché-monger has "turned off his own visual brain"
- Reader's mind stops updating the virtual reality display
- Inevitably leads to **mixed metaphors**

### Examples of Mixed Metaphors:
- "The price of chicken wings, the company's bread and butter, had risen"
- "Leica had been coasting on its laurels"
- "Microsoft began a low-octane swan song"
- "Jeff is a renaissance man, drilling down to core issues and pushing the envelope"
- "Unless you bite the bullet, you'll shoot yourself in the foot"
- "How low can the team sink? Sky's the limit!"

### Reviving Shopworn Images:
Keep idiom in the reader's mind's eye by remembering what it literally refers to:

**Original:** "When Americans are told about foreign politics, their eyes glaze over."
**Revived:** "Ever tried to explain Slovakian coalition politics to a New Yorker? He almost needed an adrenaline shot to come out of the coma."

**Original:** "Electronic publication is scholarship on steroids."
**Revived:** "With electronic publication, you can see your stuff published 15 seconds after you write it. It's scholarship on methamphetamines. Publication for speed freaks."

## Self-Conscious Writing

### Don't Whinge About Difficulty:
Self-conscious writers complain about how hard their subject is:
- "Intractability is a controversial concept, which means different things to different people"
- "Resilience is a complex multidimensional construct. Although there is no universally accepted definition..."

**Classic style response:**
- Credit the reader with enough intelligence to realize many concepts aren't easy to define
- The reader is there to see what you'll do about it, not to hear apologies

### Confident Presentation ≠ Arrogant Insistence:
- Acknowledge disagreement: "Many physicists think string theory is unproven"
- You only want readers to understand, not believe
- Present with confidence; let readers judge

### Writing as If You Have Something to Show:
- "They do not hide the passion and relish that drive them to tell us about their subjects"
- "They write as if they have something important to show" (not "say")
- This distinction is central to effective prose

## Grammar: Rules vs. Myths

### Myths to Ignore:
- "Never split an infinitive" — **Myth**. "To boldly go" is fine
- "Never end a sentence with a preposition" — **Myth**. "That's something I won't put up with"
- "Never begin with a conjunction" — **Myth**. And sometimes it adds punch
- "Never use 'which' restrictively" — **Myth** in practice
- "Passive voice is always wrong" — **Myth**. It has legitimate uses

### When Passive Voice Is Good:
- When the agent is unknown or irrelevant
- When maintaining topic continuity
- When the patient is more important than the agent

### Real Rules Worth Keeping:
- Subject-verb agreement
- Pronoun-antecedent clarity
- Dangling modifiers (fix them)
- Parallelism in lists

## The Writer's Attitude

- Write as if you have something important to **show**
- Don't hide passion for your subject
- Treat the reader as intelligent but uninformed
- Visualize yourself in conversation with the reader
- Trust readers' common sense
- Accept that good writing requires cognitive toil and literary dexterity

## Quick Checklist

Before finalizing any piece:

1. **Learning**: Am I reverse-engineering good writers' techniques?
2. **Opening**: Does it start with something worth reading—a paradox or contentful observation?
3. **Curse of Knowledge**: Will a fresh reader understand this?
4. **Abstractions**: Can any be replaced with concrete examples or images?
5. **Word Choice**: Any clichés, metaconcepts, unnecessary intensifiers, or wrong scare quotes?
6. **Phonesthetics**: Do key words echo their meaning in sound?
7. **Sentence Flow**: Heavy after light? Left-branching tangles? Center-embedded phrases?
8. **Garden Paths**: Any syntactic ambiguities that force backtracking?
9. **Coherence**: Given-new contract honored? Topics consistent? Point stated early?
10. **Parallelism**: In comparisons, is syntax identical except for the difference?
11. **Metadiscourse**: Am I focusing on subject, not my writing process? Is signposting natural?
12. **Negations**: Any multiple negations that overwhelm the reader?
13. **Hedging**: Is hedging a choice, not a tic?
14. **Concrete Details**: Showing rather than telling?
15. **Clichés**: Any that break the reader's mental visualization?
16. **Endings**: Do sentences and paragraphs end strong? Any powerful short paragraphs?
17. **Rhythm**: Good variation in sentence length?
18. **Analogies**: Are examples exact instances, not loose metaphors?

## Examples of Transformation

### Before (Academese):
"The implementation of the organizational restructuring initiative resulted in the achievement of significant improvements in operational efficiency metrics."

### After (Classic Style):
"After we reorganized, the team worked faster."

---

### Before (Zombie Nouns):
"There was a presentation given by the committee about the documentation of the procedures."

### After:
"The committee presented how we document procedures."

---

### Before (Left-Branching):
"Many of the senior researchers who had worked at the lab for over a decade and who had published extensively in the field's top journals disagreed."

### After:
"Many senior researchers disagreed—those who had worked at the lab for over a decade and published extensively in top journals."

---

### Before (Garden Path):
"The data point to increasing benefits with this experimental treatment approach for patients."

### After:
"The data point to increasing benefits for patients who receive this experimental treatment."

---

### Before (Violates Rule of One Variable):
"Children's knowledge of tool use could result from experience, but also object affordances may provide cues such that humans don't require much experimenting."

### After:
"Children's knowledge of tool use could result from their experience; alternatively, it could result from their perceiving the tool's affordances."

---

### Before (Excessive Hedging + Weak Intensifiers):
"It could be argued that Shakespeare was a somewhat important writer who would appear to have very possibly influenced the subsequent development of literature in ways that might be considered relatively significant."

### After:
"Shakespeare shaped everything that came after him."

---

### Before (Metadiscourse + Signposting):
"The following section will examine three topics. First, we will discuss the rise of the internet. Second, we will analyze its impact. Third, we will consider its future. The internet has fundamentally changed communication."

### After:
"The internet began as a military project but became a global network. It transformed communication from one-to-many (broadcast) to many-to-many (network). Now we face a choice: will it become more open and diverse, or more centralized and controlled?"

---

### Before (Cliché):
"When it comes to business growth, we need to get the ball rolling and think outside the box so we can hit it out of the park and reach for the low-hanging fruit."

### After:
"Our competitors capture low-hanging fruit first. We must identify the trees they overlook—risky ventures with potential for explosive growth."

---

### Before (Scare Quotes):
"The company has demonstrated a 'commitment' to 'innovation' and 'customer satisfaction.'"

### After:
"The company has committed to innovation and customer satisfaction—though actions matter more than words."

## Forbidden Words (user preference, 2026-08-27)

Never write: "inert", "headline" (adjective), "load-bearing", "rungs",
"conflates", "probe", "readout", "episode", "asserted", "unasserted",
"concede", "lever". Common substitutes: sensor / investigates / the
named diagnostic (probe, readout), case (episode),
declared (asserted), state the loss plainly (concede), name the
mechanism directly or "ingredient"/"factor" (lever). Avoid
self-deprecating scope disclaimers ("not a claim of X"); state results
and limits precisely instead.
