## How to use this file

When the user asks about pronunciation, or you notice they're producing a word that's likely to be mispronounced by a Vietnamese speaker, consult this file to find:
1. The relevant trap (consonant, vowel, stress, ending)
2. A concrete fix or articulation hint
3. Minimal pairs to drill the contrast

Always show IPA in slashes. Use General American (GA) by default unless the user asks for British (RP).

## IPA basics for English

**Consonants the user will already have** (Vietnamese-friendly): /p b t d k ɡ m n ŋ f v s z h l w j/

**Consonants Vietnamese does NOT have natively** (high-error zones):
- /θ/ — *think*, *both* — voiceless dental fricative, tongue between teeth
- /ð/ — *this*, *mother* — voiced dental fricative
- /ʃ/ — *she*, *wash* — voiceless postalveolar (Vietnamese has /s/ /x/ but not /ʃ/ in northern dialect; southern *s* is closer)
- /ʒ/ — *measure*, *vision* — voiced version of /ʃ/, very rare
- /tʃ/ — *church* — actually present in Vietnamese as *ch*, usually fine
- /dʒ/ — *judge* — Vietnamese speakers often substitute /j/ or /z/
- /r/ (English /ɹ/) — *red*, *very* — English uses an approximant; Vietnamese *r* is a tap or trill, sounds wrong
- /z/ — *zoo*, *please* — northern Vietnamese has /z/ (written *d*, *gi*, *r*) but speakers often devoice in final position

**Vowels — the hardest area for Vietnamese learners**, because English has ~15 vowels and Vietnamese has fewer with different distribution:
- /iː/ vs /ɪ/ — *sheep* vs *ship* — length AND tenseness differ
- /uː/ vs /ʊ/ — *pool* vs *pull*
- /æ/ — *cat* — does not exist in Vietnamese; speakers default to /e/ or /a/
- /ʌ/ — *cup* — easy to confuse with /ɑː/ or /ɒ/
- /ə/ schwa — *about*, *sofa* — the most common English vowel; in Vietnamese, every syllable carries stress, so schwa feels wrong
- /ɜː/ /ɝ/ — *bird*, *work* — r-coloured vowel, no Vietnamese equivalent
- /eɪ/ /oʊ/ /aɪ/ /aʊ/ /ɔɪ/ — diphthongs; usually OK but speakers may flatten them

## The big pronunciation traps for Vietnamese speakers

### 1. Final consonants

Vietnamese permits very few final consonants and they are unreleased: /p t k m n ŋ/ only, and /p t k/ are unreleased. English has lots of final consonants and they must be released.

- **Final /s/, /z/, /ʃ/, /tʃ/, /dʒ/, /v/, /ð/, /θ/, /f/, /l/, /r/** — all difficult
- *six* /sɪks/ often becomes /sɪt/
- *please* /pliːz/ often becomes /pliː/
- *love* /lʌv/ often becomes /lʌp/
- *with* /wɪð/ often becomes /wɪt/
- *milk* /mɪlk/ often becomes /mɪ/

**Fix**: explicitly release the final consonant. Drill: *cat / cats / catches / catch / cap*.

### 2. Final consonant clusters

Vietnamese has zero final clusters. English has many: *texts* /teksts/, *asked* /æskt/, *world* /wɜːrld/.

**Common errors**:
- *asked* → "ask" (drops -ed)
- *world* → "wor" (drops -ld)
- *first* → "firs" (drops -t)
- *helped* → "help" (drops -ed)

**Fix**: practise the cluster slowly, then speed up. The /-ed/ ending follows rules: voiceless ending → /t/ (*helped*), voiced ending → /d/ (*played*), /t/ or /d/ ending → /ɪd/ (*wanted*).

### 3. /θ/ and /ð/

Vietnamese has neither. Speakers substitute /t/ or /s/ for /θ/, and /d/ or /z/ for /ð/.

- *think* /θɪŋk/ → "tink" or "sink"
- *this* /ðɪs/ → "dis" or "zis"
- *three* /θriː/ → "tree"

**Fix**: tongue tip lightly between the teeth, blow gently. *Three free trees through the thicket.* Visible mirror-check helps.

### 4. /r/ as approximant, not trill/tap

English /ɹ/ (often written /r/) is an approximant: tongue curls back without touching anywhere. Vietnamese *r* is a tap (southern) or rolled (some dialects) or even /z/ (northern).

- *red* /ɹɛd/ — sounds wrong as a tapped *red*
- *very* /ˈvɛɹi/

**Fix**: imagine making a /w/ but with the tongue curled up; no contact with the roof of the mouth.

### 5. /l/ vs /n/

Some Vietnamese speakers (especially northern) confuse initial /l/ and /n/.
- *late* vs *Nate*
- *light* vs *night*

**Fix**: /l/ — tongue tip touches alveolar ridge, air flows around the sides; /n/ — tongue tip touches alveolar ridge, air flows through the nose. Hold a finger under the nose: /n/ vibrates the finger.

### 6. /æ/ does not exist

*cat* /kæt/, *bag* /bæɡ/, *man* /mæn/, *apple* /ˈæpəl/. Vietnamese speakers default to /e/ (sounds like *ket*, *beg*, *men*) or /a/ (sounds like *cot*).

**Fix**: open mouth wider than for /e/, jaw lower, smile slightly. Drill *cat / cot / cut / caught*.

### 7. Long vs short vowels

English distinguishes /iː/–/ɪ/, /uː/–/ʊ/, /ɔː/–/ɒ/. Vietnamese has length distinctions but at different places.
- *sheep* /ʃiːp/ vs *ship* /ʃɪp/
- *pool* /puːl/ vs *pull* /pʊl/
- *leave* /liːv/ vs *live* /lɪv/

**Fix**: short vowels /ɪ ʊ ɛ æ ʌ ɒ/ are *quick and lax*; long vowels /iː uː ɔː ɑː ɜː/ are *longer and tenser*.

### 8. Schwa /ə/

Unstressed syllables in English collapse to /ə/. Vietnamese has no schwa; every syllable is fully articulated.
- *banana* /bəˈnænə/ — only the middle syllable is full
- *photograph* /ˈfoʊtəɡræf/, *photographer* /fəˈtɑɡrəfər/, *photographic* /ˌfoʊtəˈɡræfɪk/ — note how the same letters reduce differently depending on stress

**Fix**: under-articulate unstressed syllables. The user will overpronounce by default — tell them to be lazier.

### 9. The /v/–/w/–/b/ triangle

Vietnamese *v* (north) is /v/; (south) is more like /j/. English /v/ requires top teeth on lower lip with vibration. Some speakers also blur /v/ and /w/.
- *very* /ˈvɛɹi/ vs *wary* /ˈwɛɹi/
- *vest* vs *west*

### 10. Word-final voicing affects vowel length

In English, the vowel *before* a voiced final consonant is longer than before a voiceless one.
- *bat* /bæt/ has shorter /æ/ than *bad* /bæd/
- *back* shorter than *bag*
- *cap* shorter than *cab*

Vietnamese speakers who can't produce the final voicing contrast can still cue it via vowel length.

## Stress and rhythm

English is stress-timed. Vietnamese is syllable-timed (every syllable roughly equal). This is the *single biggest* reason Vietnamese-accented English sounds non-native even when the segments are correct.

Rules of thumb:
- **Stressed syllable** is louder, longer, higher in pitch, and with a full vowel
- **Unstressed syllables** are quieter, shorter, lower, and reduce to /ə/ or /ɪ/
- Two-syllable nouns: usually first-syllable stress (*RECord, OBject, PREsent* as nouns)
- Two-syllable verbs: usually second-syllable stress (*reCORD, obJECT, preSENT* as verbs)
- Compounds: usually stress on the first part (*GREENhouse, BLACKboard*)
- Phrases: usually stress on the second part (*green HOUSE = a house painted green*)

Always mark stress in IPA: `/ˈsʌm.θɪŋ/`. The `ˈ` precedes the primary stressed syllable; `ˌ` precedes a secondary stressed syllable.

## Connected speech

Native English fuses words together. The user, hearing carefully-pronounced isolated words, won't catch fast speech.

- **Linking**: *turn off* → "tur-noff"; *not at all* → "no-da-dall"
- **/t/ flapping (US)**: between vowels, /t/ becomes a flap [ɾ], close to /d/ — *water* sounds like "wadder"
- **Reduction**: *want to* → *wanna*, *going to* → *gonna*, *got to* → *gotta*, *kind of* → *kinda*
- **Schwa-ing of function words**: *to* /tə/, *for* /fər/, *and* /ən/, *of* /əv/ or /ə/, *can* /kən/

When teaching a phrase, give both the careful and the connected pronunciation:
- *I have to go.* → careful: /aɪ hæv tu ɡoʊ/, natural: /aɪ ˈhæftə ɡoʊ/

## Diagnostic shortcuts

When the user says a word and asks if their pronunciation is right, mentally check:
1. **All final consonants released?**
2. **Stress on the right syllable?**
3. **Schwa on the unstressed syllables, or full vowels?**
4. **Any /θ ð r æ/ that might have been swapped?**
5. **Long/short vowel correct?**

If yes to all, it's good. If not, fix the highest-impact one first — usually stress or final consonants.
