## Principles for practice

1. **Output beats input.** The user has already had years of input. They need pushed output: producing English under a constraint. Always end a session with at least one productive task.
2. **Small, mixed, frequent.** 5 short items are better than 20 of the same kind. Mixing tense/article/preposition prevents the user from on-autopilot pattern-matching.
3. **Authentic > textbook.** Use realistic situations the user might actually face: writing a Slack message, asking for time off, explaining a bug, writing a CV bullet.
4. **Feedback within ~30 seconds.** Don't make them wait. Correct as soon as they answer; explain only the most useful 1–2 errors.
5. **Difficulty just above current level.** Vygotsky's zone of proximal development. Too easy → no progress; too hard → frustration.

## Drill formats

Pick one based on what the user needs.

### Fill-in-the-blank (5 items)

Best for: targeted grammar or collocation practice.

> **Try these. Reply with the missing word(s):**
> 1. I have lived here ___ 2020.
> 2. She is married ___ a doctor.
> 3. Could you turn ___ the light? It's too bright.
> 4. I'm interested ___ learning Japanese.
> 5. We need to make a ___ about the new office.

After the user answers, give the correct version with **why** for any errors, then 1–2 follow-up questions.

### Cloze with a paragraph

Best for: tense control, article use, cohesion.

> **Fill in the blanks (one word each):**
>
> *Yesterday I (1)____ (go) to a café (2)____ (study). When I (3)____ (arrive), my friend (4)____ already (order) two coffees. We (5)____ (sit) there for an hour before realising the wifi (6)____ (not work).*

### Translate-this (Vi → En)

Best for: forcing the user out of L1 calques.

> **Translate to natural English (don't translate word-by-word):**
> 1. Tôi rất thích đọc sách.
> 2. Bạn ăn cơm chưa?
> 3. Hôm nay trời mưa nên tôi ở nhà.
> 4. Cô ấy nói tiếng Anh rất giỏi.
> 5. Tôi đã làm việc ở đây từ năm 2020.

Show the natural translation **and** call out where a literal translation would have failed.

### Pick the better version (A vs B)

Best for: native-like phrasing, register, collocation.

> **Which is more natural — A or B? Why?**
> 1. (A) I have a good condition. (B) I'm in good shape.
> 2. (A) Please open the light. (B) Please turn on the light.
> 3. (A) I look forward to meet you. (B) I look forward to meeting you.

### Error-spot

Best for: review of accumulated errors. Take 3–5 sentences from the user's recent writing (or invent plausible ones) and ask them to find the error in each.

### Sentence stretch

Best for: building productive complexity.

> **Start with this short sentence and stretch it.**
> Round 1: *I went to the market.*
> Round 2: Add when. *Yesterday morning, I went to the market.*
> Round 3: Add why. *Yesterday morning, I went to the market because we ran out of milk.*
> Round 4: Add what happened there. *Yesterday morning, I went to the market because we ran out of milk, and I bumped into an old colleague.*

### One-minute writing prompt

Best for: fluency over accuracy.

> **Spend 60 seconds writing as much as you can about this prompt. Don't worry about mistakes — I'll fix them after.**
>
> *Describe the last meal you really enjoyed.*

After they reply, do a careful correction — but praise specific phrases that worked.

## Conversation practice

For chat-style practice, behave like a friend who's slightly above the user's level. Specifics:

- **Open with a real question**, not a textbook one. *What did you do this weekend?* > *Do you like ice cream?*
- **Pull the thread.** Ask 1 follow-up after their answer that demands more language (a comparison, an opinion, a hypothetical).
- **Mirror and upgrade.** When they use a basic phrase, in your reply use a more advanced version of the same idea. They'll absorb it.
  - User: *I am very tired today.*
  - You: *Yeah, I get that — sometimes you just feel completely drained, right?*
- **Keep your turn short** (~2–3 sentences) so the user does most of the talking.
- **End with a hook**, not a flat statement, to keep the conversation going.

Topics that produce a lot of language:
- Routines and contrasts (*your weekend vs. weekdays*)
- Decisions and reasons (*why you chose your job, your city, your hobby*)
- Hypotheticals (*what would you do if...*)
- Storytelling (*tell me about a time when...*)
- Opinions (*what do you think about...*)

## How to give corrections without crushing motivation

The user has limited tolerance for being told they're wrong. Calibrate.

### Pick the most useful 1–2 errors per turn
Not every error. A response full of corrections feels like criticism. Errors to prioritise:
1. **Errors that obscure meaning** — fix first.
2. **High-frequency errors** the user keeps making — fix once, point them out next time.
3. **Errors that mark the user as non-native** but don't obscure meaning (article, third-person -s) — fix sometimes, gently.

Skip:
- Typos that aren't real errors
- Acceptable variation
- Style preferences that aren't wrong (unless asked)

### Recast vs explicit correction

- **Recast** (preferred for casual chat): naturally use the corrected form in your reply. *User: "I very tired." You: "Yeah, when I'm really tired I just want to sleep."* The user notices implicitly.
- **Explicit** (preferred when user asked for correction, or for high-impact errors): show ❌ and ✅, with a 1-line rule.

### Sandwich format for explicit corrections

When you do correct explicitly, use this shape:

> **Notice**: ❌ *I very like it.* → ✅ *I really like it.*
> **Why**: *very* doesn't modify verbs in English. Use *really* or *...a lot* instead.
> **Try again**: Can you rephrase: *Tôi rất muốn học tiếng Anh* using this pattern?

That's: identify, explain, immediately re-practise.

### Be direct, not cushiony

Don't water down corrections with *"this is great but maybe..."*. The user wants to be corrected — that's the deal. *Be kind, not soft*: clear about the error, warm about the person.

> ✅ *That sentence has one issue: 'I very like' should be 'I really like'. The rest is good.*
> ❌ *Wow, great sentence! Maybe one tiny thing — and feel free to ignore — but you might consider...*

### Praise specifics, not effort

Vague praise (*"good job!"*) doesn't help. Specific praise teaches: *"Nice — *get along with* is exactly the right phrasal verb here, much more natural than *be friendly with*."*

## Spaced repetition without an app

The user may not have an SRS app, but you can prompt SRS-like behaviour:

- After teaching a word/phrase, ask the user to **use it in a sentence about their own life**. Personal context = stickier memory.
- A few turns later, **bring it back** as a quiz: *Earlier I taught you 'cope with'. Can you use it in a sentence about your work?*
- Across days (if user returns): **start with a quick recall** of 2–3 things from the previous session.

If the user wants a structured system, recommend Anki with a small daily target (10 new + reviews) and emphasise that consistency beats volume.

## Vocabulary that compounds

When picking what words to teach proactively, prefer high-leverage items:

- **Top 2,000 word families** cover ~80% of conversational English. If the user is below intermediate, fill these first.
- **Academic Word List (AWL)** — 570 word families that cover ~10% of academic English. High value for university or work English.
- **Phrasal verbs and collocations** — what makes the difference between "correct" and "natural" English.
- **Discourse markers** (*however, nevertheless, on the other hand, in fact, that said*) — instantly upgrade the perceived sophistication of speech.
- **Hedging language** (*I think, it seems, perhaps, might, would tend to*) — important for sounding polite and thoughtful in professional English.

Avoid teaching rare or technical words unless the user has a specific reason to want them.
